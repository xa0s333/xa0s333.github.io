## Where it started

I've basically been in front of a computer for as long as I can remember. My older brother got me into messing with games when I was really young, and by around six I was already interested in experimenting with game files, scripts and mods, and generally poking at things I didn't fully understand yet.

As I got older that turned into making scripts for different games, including OSRS, messing around with game files and figuring out how things worked behind the scenes. I also spent a lot of time disassembling different consoles, softmodding PlayStations, installing homebrew, custom software and generally trying to make hardware do things it wasn't originally meant to do.

I didn't think of any of this as "cybersecurity" at the time. I just liked understanding how things worked, finding ways around restrictions and seeing what I could make a system do.

That interest eventually carried over into programming, Linux, networking and later security. These days I mostly spend that time on `Web Security`, `OSINT`, CTFs and whatever other technical rabbit hole catches my attention.

## What I like doing

``Web security`` is probably what I spend the most
time on. I especially like challenges where the vulnerability
isn't immediately obvious and you have to understand how several
parts of the application interact before anything useful comes
out of it. Authentication, access control, weird application
logic, API behaviour and server-side bugs are generally the
things I find the most fun.

``OSINT`` is another area I really enjoy. Mostly
following small pieces of public information and figuring out
how they connect, whether that's usernames, domains, old pages,
images, metadata or other parts of someone's or something's
digital footprint.

I also like CTF challenges that force me outside of those areas.
Half the fun is getting a task where I initially have no idea
what I'm looking at and having to figure it out from there.

## Platforms

`Hack The Box`

Probably the platform I use the most overall. I now mostly spend time on machines, but used to do a lot more sherlocks in the past.

The machines are useful for getting hands-on with full attack paths rather than isolated vulnerabilities. Requiring to chaining different attack vectors together is also great practice.

I like sherlocks since they give a sort of work-balance by forcing me to slow down and actually look at evidence instead of immediately trying to break something. Logs, files, network traffic and timelines are usually a nice change of pace from normal offensive challenges.

`PortSwigger Web Security Academy`

This is where I do a lot of focused web-security practice.

I mainly use it when I want to properly understand a specific vulnerability instead of only solving whatever happens to appear in a CTF. The labs are useful for basically anything related to web exploitation. I mostly use them when I want to understand a technique properly instead of only seeing it once in a CTF and moving on.

`OSINT Industries CTF`

I use this mostly for the more investigative side of security.

The challenges are centered around following public information, linking identities and accounts together, working with usernames, images, metadata and other pieces of a digital footprint. I enjoy that kind of problem because there usually isn't one obvious tool that gives you the answer, so you have to keep finding small clues and connect them yourself.

`picoCTF`

This was one of the platforms I used more when I was getting started.

I don't spend much time on it anymore, but it was useful for getting exposed to a lot of different categories without needing much setup. It gave me a good entry point into CTFs before I started spending more time on harder standalone challenges and Hack The Box.
